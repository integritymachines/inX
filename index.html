    Seed patterns

    The stream begins with these, then evolves. The defaults are original placeholder motifs. To start from In C, paste its 53 patterns from your copy of the score: one JSON array, one object per pattern, len in eighth notes, each note as [onset, duration, MIDI pitch] with middle C = 60; use halves (0.5) for sixteenths.

    Notes ↑ Play

    1. In X. Players work through an endless, evolving stream of short patterns, following the rules of Terry Riley's In C (1964): each player repeats a pattern as often as it likes, then moves on, never back, and stays within 3 patterns of the slowest player (Terry's slider changes this span). Now and then one player calls everybody in for a crescendo; then all but one drop out, and the remaining player carries the music into a new section, plainly at first, until the section's theme brings the others back.
    2. Opening. After Start the pulse plays alone while the instrument samples load: at least 12 seconds, and until every sample is in (at most a minute); then the first player comes in.
    3. Status. Phase: flow, calling everybody in, climax (the crescendo), dropout, solo, or restart while players come back. Patterns: the lowest and highest pattern being played. Section changes after each solo; key about halfway through a section, when its new tone arrives. The gauges at the bottom of the controls: output level; visitor traffic; intensity, the current height of John's wave, with ↗ while rising (– without John); and players, how many are sounding, of 24.
    4. Pattern stream. The patterns around those in play, outlined when in play. Labels give the pattern number and how it was made: S seed, M mutation, X crossover of two parents, N new. A bar on the left edge marks a key change; the bar underneath shows excitement. Each dot below a pattern is a player on it, faint while resting. Dashed patterns to the right are coming up next: they are generated ahead, and a new section after a gathering may replace them.
    5. How patterns are chosen. Pitch content moves the way In C's does. A section opens on four tones of the key (1, 3, 4, 5, like In C's C E F G) over the tonic chord; the 7th joins, then the rest of the scale. About halfway one new tone arrives (the first, like In C's F♯, on the sharp side) and tips the key a fifth; keys wander from section to section and turn back toward home once two steps away. Chords (shown under each pattern number) stay on I through the opening, then move through I, ii, iii, IV, V and vi, faster as the build nears (every 6–10 patterns at first, every 3–5 later), and the dominant is held for the last fifth before the climax, so the climax and solo resolve it. Each new pattern is picked from 18 candidates for coherence (its weight on the current chord's tones, favored but not enforced), complexity close to a target (rhythmic variability, melodic-interval entropy, pitch range and density), kinship with its parent, and kinship with the section's theme. Each section follows an arc from the solo that opens it to the next climax (about the patterns between gatherings plus 6): the complexity target rises from plain and narrow to intricate and wide. Deliberately plain patterns (a long tone, a repeated note, two tones) are frequent in the opening fifth (about a third), return for a breath before the build, and all but vanish at the top. A section opens with the soloist on two or three plain patterns; as the soloist reaches the last of them, the first player back comes in on the next, interesting pattern, which becomes the section's theme. The rest return one by one as the arc rises: the ensemble grows from the minimum at the start of a section to about four fifths of the maximum when a gathering is due (on average about 2–3 players in the first fifth, 6 in the last), and the gathering calls in the rest, starting with those still sitting out. Early in the section half of all new patterns rework the theme, later fewer, and brand-new material appears mainly mid-section. With John the target leans a little on his wave. Pattern lengths vary as widely as In C's: each pattern draws a length target from a long-tailed spread (calibrated on In C's 53 patterns: median 6 eighth notes, about one in five 8½ quarter notes or more, up to 32 quarter notes). The pulse is a sixteenth note, so short cells (up to 8 eighths) can move in sixteenths as In C's do, about one pattern in five. Every pattern lasts a whole number of eighths and every repetition starts on an eighth, so the ensemble shares one eighth-note grid; only Steve's phasing drifts off it, by sixteenths, and snaps back. The audible pulse (the high C) stays in eighths, and candidates are stretched by sequencing a segment a step up or down, or cut to a window, to meet it. A player stays on a pattern for about the same time whatever its length, so a long pattern is repeated only once or twice. Excitement is a pattern's complexity on a fixed 0–1 scale, plain to intricate (the bar under each pattern). When this box is on, exciting patterns are held longer, coherent patterns are more likely to become parents, and a gathering's crescendo lasts longer when the patterns in play are exciting. Key changes pass through a chord both keys share.
    6. Players. Tap a tile to switch a player on or off. It enters or leaves after a short lag, at the end of a pattern; during a climax or a solo it waits. Players you switch on stay until you switch them off; simulated visitors leave when their lease runs out. Each tile shows the player's state, its pattern and repetition (p·r), its lease, and its progress through the current repetition. 24 players: keyboards, strings, woodwinds, brass and percussion, seated across the stereo field roughly as in an orchestra. Hand percussion is unpitched: it plays its pattern's rhythm, and each note's register picks the drum, from low log drum and conga to cowbell and claves.
    7. Events. The line under the players: players joining and leaving, gatherings, phasing and additive episodes, new sections, newest first on the left.
    8. Composers. Terry (Riley): In C's rules, always on, since everything builds on them; his slider sets the widest span between the slowest and the fastest player, in patterns (In C: about 3). At 1 the ensemble moves almost in lockstep; at 8 it spreads into a wide canon. Each player has its own pace (lingering up to twice as long as average, or pressing on in half the time), and the pull toward the middle of the ensemble weakens as the span widens, so the players actually use it: on average they spread over about three fifths of the span. The other four can be switched on or off on their own, at any time. Steve (Reich) phasing: a player on the same pattern as another speeds up until it is one pulse ahead, locks there, and repeats a few times. Philip (Glass) additive process: a player builds its pattern up one note group at a time, then takes it apart again. John (Adams) intensity waves: a slow wave (2.5–4 minutes) moves loudness, brightness, rests, pulsing repeated notes and octave doubling together, and gatherings tend to crest with it. La Monte (Young) just intonation and drone: pure intervals on the current key, retuned in one step at each key change, over a drone of the tonic with its fifth, octave, twelfth and natural seventh. Switching a technique off ends it gently: a phasing player snaps back to the pulse, a growing pattern returns to full, the wave settles at middle intensity, the drone fades.
    9. Depth sliders. Pulse: the level of the high C in eighth notes (50% is the original level, 0 switches it off; it fades out anyway as Techno's hats come in). Philip: how often a player starts the additive process and how many run it at once (50% is the original setting: 12% of new patterns, two players). Steve: how often phasing starts and how short the quiet between episodes is (50%: original). John: how far his waves move loudness, brightness, rests, pulsing repeated notes, octave doubling, the complexity target and the groove's density (70% is the original setting). La Monte: the drone's level; at 0 the just intonation stays and the drone falls silent. Groove evolution: how much the drums change, scaling fills, ghost notes, intensity-driven density, the small drift every phrase, and the new groove for each section; near 0 the groove stays as the preset sets it. A slider is greyed out while it has nothing to act on: a composer's while he is switched off, groove evolution while there are no drums (Techno below 20%).
    10. Techno. One slider moves In X from orchestra toward techno. The tempo stays as set; from 25% the tempo slider reads in BPM, the pulse counting as a sixteenth note. from 15%, players enter on the beat. Then a synthesized rhythm section comes in, a voice at a time: kick at 20%, closed hats at 35%, clap at 40% (the pulse fades out as the hats arrive), open hats at 45%, percussion at 55%, and at 60% a bass line on the root of the current chord, with the orchestra pumping under each kick (sidechain ducking, deepening up to 100%). From 50%, players enter on the bar and the foundation group locks to strict 4/4: their patterns are padded to 4, 8, 16 or 32 steps and always start on the beat. Line and color players keep In C's free pattern lengths, so they run in polymeter against the grid. The grid below the stream shows the current bar of the drums. The groove has shape: hats and percussion thin out in quiet stretches and fill in at intense ones (John's intensity, or Terry's slow shaping), ghost notes come and go, the last bar of an 8-bar phrase often brings a fill (a snare roll, a dropped kick, an open-hat lift, a percussion run), the patterns drift a little every phrase, and each new section gets a new groove. From 50%, a gathering takes techno's form: calling everybody in is a breakdown (kick and bass out, the orchestra filtered down), the climax is a build (a snare roll speeding up to thirty-seconds, a noise riser, the filter opening), and the dropout begins with the kick slamming back under a crash; the groove carries on through the dropout and the solo.
    11. Electronic voices. Each player also has a synthesized voice for club styles: the pianos become stabs, the organ and upper strings pads, the low strings and bassoon a detuned "reese" bass, double bass and tuba a sub bass, harp and guitar plucks, the winds leads, the brass brass stabs, the mallets FM bells, the timpani toms and the hand percussion clicks. Players crossfade from their recordings to these voices as the electronic mix rises: each preset sets its own mix (Berghain, Techno and Acid fully electronic; House and Panorama Bar about half; Wilde Renate mostly acoustic); custom settings blend in from 70% Techno. The electronic voices are balanced by measurement, like the recordings.
    12. Presets and tempo. The menu lists general scenes first (In X (orchestra), Minimal, Techno, Acid, House, Chillwave, Middle and Random), then the Berlin clubs, then the modes. A preset is a starting point, and it stays shown while you change controls, until you choose another. Middle puts every control at the middle of its range with all composers on at half depth; Random draws a fresh set of settings each time you choose it (double-click the menu to draw again). A preset sets the Techno amount, the tempo, a drum kit (kick length and pitch, hat lengths, clap, snare or rim, the bass type, swing, how deeply the orchestra pumps, how busy hats and percussion may get), the reverb, which composers play, and the largest ensemble. Minimal: clicky, sparse, rim shots, light swing. Berghain: 136 BPM, long dark kick, no clap, rolling rumble bass, John's waves over La Monte's drone. Panorama Bar and House: around 122–124 BPM, swung hats, open hats on the off-beat, a rounded plucked bass. Wilde Renate: playful, percussive, Steve and Philip at work. Tresor: hard, dark and mechanical, 134 BPM, long kick, rumbling bass, metallic percussion, no clap, Steve's phasing grinding away in a reverberant basement. KitKat: fast and hedonistic, 138 BPM, acid bass, open hats, John's waves at full depth. Kater Blau: warm and loose by the river, 120 BPM, swung house bass and claps, lots of percussion, the orchestra still well in, Philip and Steve at play. Club der Visionäre: minimal and dry under the willow, 123 BPM, rimshots, sparse hats, busy small percussion, Steve's phasing. Sisyphos: a long, sunny marathon, 125 BPM, swung house bass, plenty of percussion, Philip and John at work. Each club preset brings its own fixed line-up, and it stays: Berghain low brass, bassoon, cellos, violas, organ and timpani; Panorama Bar two pianos, organ, violins, sax, trumpet, marimba, harp, bass and hand percussion; Wilde Renate mallets, piccolo, flute, clarinet, guitar, piano, bass and hand percussion; Tresor tuba, trombone, horn, organ, cellos, bass, timpani and xylophone; KitKat sax, trumpet, trombone, piano, organ, violins, flute, piccolo, glockenspiel, xylophone, bass and hand percussion; Kater Blau sixteen, strings, winds, guitar, harp, mallets and hand percussion; Club der Visionäre ten, piano, guitar, harp, viola, clarinet, flute, marimba, glockenspiel, bass and hand percussion; Sisyphos fourteen, strings, piano, guitar, sax, trumpet, horn, flute, oboe, harp, marimba and hand percussion. You can still switch any player on or off. Techno: straight 130. Acid: a resonant sawtooth bass with accents and slides. Chillwave: 92 BPM, half-time kick and snare, lots of reverb, slow waves. Club names are nods to their sound. Everything stays adjustable; changing anything shows "Custom". There is one tempo. Below 25% Techno the slider shows it as milliseconds per eighth note, above it as BPM (a quarter note = two eighths, so 200 ms = 150 BPM, 234 ms = 128 BPM); moving the Techno slider never changes it.
    13. Modes (ADHD). Three presets set In X up for a kind of work: Productive, Creative and Focus. Productive: a fixed ensemble of eight (double bass, cellos, Piano I, violins, flute, clarinet, marimba, harp) that comes in and stays, with no visitors coming and going; 115 BPM in strict 4/4 with every player locked to the grid, a minimal kit, a tight ensemble (span 2), no extra composers, little new material, the new tone late in a section, gatherings rare (80 patterns apart) and gentle (a 2 dB climax), and Terry's slow level shaping mostly flattened. Creative: orchestral, slow (an eighth note every 400 ms, 75 BPM), free meters, all composers, a wide span (7), many players, frequent gatherings with full climaxes, more new material, and now and then a key that leaps two or three fifths at once instead of one. Focus (a cold-start ramp): starts with a fixed ensemble of fourteen (Productive's eight plus timpani, grand piano, trumpet, saxophone, xylophone and hand percussion) at 128 BPM with a full groove and deep waves, calls everybody in for a full climax about two and a half minutes in, then glides every setting (tempo, groove, span, waves, gathering spacing and height, surprise) into Productive's steady settings over ten minutes; over the second half the six extra players leave one by one. Creative keeps the open roster, with visitors coming and going. Switching to any other preset hands a mode's fixed players back to ordinary leases. Moving any control during the glide stops it and leaves everything as it stands.
    14. Players (min–max). One slider with two handles. The left handle (up to 12): the ensemble never gets smaller than this, except in the solo after a gathering. Any instrument can fill a gap, and a lease can't end if that would take the ensemble below the minimum.
    15. Maximum players (the right handle). The most players sounding at once, gatherings included: a gathering calls in idle players only up to this number. If you switch a player on when the ensemble is full, a simulated visitor leaves to make room. Overall gain also eases down as the ensemble grows, to keep the climax clear of overload; lower this number if your device still struggles.
    16. Stay per pattern. The average time a player spends on a pattern before moving on, from 5 s to 2 min (each player varies around it with its own pace, and Excitement lengthens the stay on exciting patterns). A live performance of In C takes its 53 patterns in about an hour, roughly a minute each; the In X (orchestra) preset uses 60 s, the club presets 20–30 s. Gatherings and section arcs count patterns, so a longer stay stretches them in time too.
    17. Visitor traffic. Simulated site visitors switch players on at random, keep them for a lease, then switch them off; they arrive faster when traffic is high, but no faster than the section's arc allows (see How patterns are chosen). The meter drifts slowly and sometimes spikes. When a gathering is called, idle players you haven't switched off are called in, up to the maximum.
    18. Visitor lease. How long a simulated visitor keeps a player: from half to one and a half times the middle value shown. Leases don't run out during a gathering. Players you switch on yourself have no lease.
    19. Patterns between gatherings. The least number of patterns between gatherings. After that the chance of a call rises steadily; with Adams waves, calls favor a rising wave.
    20. Balance, groups and dynamics. Every instrument's level was measured from offline renders, separately for short and held notes, and corrected so that all 24 are equally loud at the same dynamic. The players form three groups: foundation (double bass, cellos, tuba, trombone, bassoon, timpani), slightly below the middle; line (pianos, organ, violins, violas, guitar, flute, oboe, clarinet, saxophone, horn, trumpet), at the middle; and color (harp, piccolo, marimba, xylophone, glockenspiel, hand percussion), a few dB below. Arrivals favor whichever group is missing or short, aiming at one foundation to two line to one color, so outside solos all three are almost always present. A slow ensemble-wide gain keeps the overall level on its planned course however many players there are and however busy their patterns: a full ensemble may be up to 3 dB louder than two players, but no more. On that steady base, Terry alone shapes the level slowly (about ±3 dB over a few minutes); with John, his waves take over; from the moment a gathering is called until the solo ends, this gain holds still, so arrivals and the climax make the music louder. The climax (24–40 s) rises from the current level to +7 dB, reaches it at 70% of the way and holds it; the dropout (14–24 s) fades each leaving player gently.
    21. Recorded instruments. The players use short recordings of real instruments, loaded in the background after you press Start; until an instrument has loaded, a synthesized voice stands in. A note uses the nearest recorded pitch, played slightly faster or slower, so La Monte's just intonation still applies. Recordings: VSCO 2 Community Edition by Versilian Studios, Sam Gossner and Simon Dalzell (CC0); the grand piano (a Steinway B), congas and bongos, darbuka and frame drums, and the bells and woods (agogô, cowbell, claves, woodblock, triangle) from the Versilian Community Sample Library (CC0); saxophone and guitar from the Tone.js instrument samples by Nicholas Brosowsky (CC BY 3.0), originally from Karoryfer Samples and the University of Iowa Electronic Music Studios.
    22. Setup. The second screen chooses the instruments on stage (up to 24 of the 27 available, at least 2) and the seed patterns. Beyond the default 24 there are three percussion players: congas and bongos, darbuka and frame drum, and bells and woods; like the hand percussion, each picks one of six sounds by the register of the note it plays. Changing the line-up takes effect at once: kept instruments keep their players, new ones appear as empty tiles, and players whose instrument you take off finish their pattern and leave. A club preset or mode with its own line-up swaps any missing instrument onto the stage in place of one it doesn't use.
